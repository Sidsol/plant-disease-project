# Tomato Mosaic Virus (ToMV)

## Overview
Tomato mosaic virus (ToMV) is a highly stable tobamovirus that causes mosaic patterns and distortion on tomato foliage. It is closely related to tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) and can cause significant economic losses. The virus is extremely stable and can persist on contaminated surfaces, tools, and hands for extended periods.

## Symptoms
- **Leaves**: Mottled light green and dark green mosaic pattern. Leaves may be distorted, curled, or reduced in size ("fern-leaf" symptom in severe infections). Young leaves show the most dramatic symptoms.
- **Fruit**: Internal browning ("brown wall"), uneven ripening, and occasionally raised, necrotic spots on the surface. Fruit may be unmarketable due to mottling and poor quality.
- **Growth**: Stunted growth, especially when infection occurs early. Yield losses of 10-50% are possible depending on the cultivar and timing of infection.
- **Temperature sensitivity**: Symptom expression varies with temperature — more severe at 68-77°F (20-25°C).

## Disease Cycle
1. The virus is extremely stable and can survive for decades in dried plant material, on contaminated surfaces, and in soil.
2. Mechanical transmission is the primary spread mechanism: contaminated hands, tools, clothes, and equipment spread the virus from plant to plant.
3. Seed transmission occurs at low rates (< 1% but sufficient to introduce the virus).
4. The virus does not have an insect vector — this distinguishes it from many other tomato viruses.
5. Infected root fragments in soil can infect subsequent plantings.

## Treatment

### No Chemical Cure
- No chemical treatment can cure virus infections. Once a plant is infected, it remains infected for life.
- Management focuses on prevention and limiting spread.

### Cultural Practices
- Remove and destroy infected plants immediately to prevent further spread.
- **Disinfect tools** between plants using one of these methods (ranked by effectiveness):
  - 20% nonfat dry milk solution (soak or dip tools — the milk proteins bind and inactivate the virus)
  - 70% isopropyl alcohol (dip for 30 seconds; preferred over bleach as it's less corrosive to tools)
  - 10% bleach solution (1 part bleach to 9 parts water; rinse and oil tools afterward to prevent corrosion)
- Wash hands thoroughly with soap, or dip in milk solution, before handling healthy plants after touching any suspect material.
- Plant TMV/ToMV-resistant varieties — the Tm-2 and Tm-2² resistance genes provide excellent protection against most strains. **This is by far the most reliable control measure.**
- Use certified virus-free seed from reputable suppliers.
- Do not smoke or use tobacco products near tomato plants — tobacco can carry TMV, and the virus transfers easily from hands to plants.
- Remove all crop debris and roots at end of season. The virus can survive in dry plant material for decades.
- Do not save seed from infected or suspect plants.

**Real-world context**: TMV is extraordinarily stable. It can survive on contaminated greenhouse surfaces, wooden stakes, plant ties, and even clothing for months to years. Unlike most pathogens, TMV has no insect vector — it spreads entirely by mechanical contact. A single brief touch of an infected plant followed by handling healthy plants can transmit the virus. This makes sanitation the most critical management tool after variety resistance.

## Prevention
- **Resistance is the best strategy**: plant varieties carrying Tm-2² resistance gene.
- Use certified, virus-tested seed from reputable sources.
- Start with clean transplants from reputable nurseries.
- Sanitize greenhouses and equipment between crops.
- Educate workers about the importance of hand and tool sanitation.
