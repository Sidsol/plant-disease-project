# Peach Bacterial Spot (Xanthomonas arboricola pv. pruni)

## Overview
Bacterial spot of peach is caused by *Xanthomonas arboricola* pv. *pruni*. It is a serious disease in warm, humid climates, causing defoliation, fruit blemishes, and twig cankers. The disease is particularly damaging to susceptible cultivars and can make fruit unmarketable. Nectarines are generally more susceptible than peaches.

## Symptoms
- **Leaves**: Small (1-3 mm), angular, water-soaked spots appear, typically concentrated near the leaf tip. Spots turn purple-brown and may develop a yellow halo. Severely infected leaves develop a tattered or "shot-hole" appearance as necrotic tissue falls out. Premature defoliation is common.
- **Fruit**: Small, dark, sunken spots develop into deep pits or cracks. Spots may coalesce and cause large, cracked, gummy lesions. Fruit is susceptible from shuck-split through maturity.
- **Twigs**: Dark, sunken, spring cankers form on previous season's growth, serving as inoculum sources. Cankers exude bacterial ooze during wet weather.

## Disease Cycle
1. The bacterium overwinters in twig cankers and on bud surfaces.
2. In spring, rain splash disperses bacteria from cankers to young leaves and developing fruit.
3. Wind-driven rain is particularly effective at spreading the pathogen.
4. Warm temperatures (75-86°F / 24-30°C) and frequent rainfall favor disease development.
5. Infection is through stomata and wounds; no insect vector is required.

## Treatment

### Chemical Control
- Apply copper sprays at leaf fall and again at bud swell (dormant to green-tip stage).
- Oxytetracycline (Mycoshield) can be applied during the growing season at 7-10 day intervals during wet weather.
- Copper is phytotoxic to peach during the growing season — restrict to dormant applications.
- Combination of copper + mancozeb (dormant) provides broad-spectrum protection.

### Cultural Practices
- Plant resistant cultivars: Contender, Biscoe, and Harrow Diamond show good resistance.
- Avoid overhead irrigation that keeps foliage wet.
- Orient rows with prevailing winds to facilitate rapid canopy drying.
- Prune to improve air circulation.
- Remove and destroy heavily cankered shoots during dormant pruning.
- Avoid excessive nitrogen fertilization, which promotes susceptible new growth.

## Prevention
- Cultivar selection is the most effective long-term strategy.
- Site selection: avoid low-lying areas where moisture accumulates.
- Maintain tree vigor through balanced fertility and proper irrigation.
- Use windbreaks cautiously — they can reduce wind-driven rain but also reduce canopy drying.
