# Squash Powdery Mildew (Podosphaera xanthii / Erysiphe cichoracearum)

## Overview
Powdery mildew is the most common disease of cucurbits (squash, pumpkin, cucumber, melon). It is caused primarily by *Podosphaera xanthii* (syn. *Sphaerotheca fuliginea*), with *Erysiphe cichoracearum* (syn. *Golovinomyces cichoracearum*) also causing the disease in some regions. Unlike most fungal diseases, powdery mildew can thrive in warm, dry conditions and does not require leaf wetness for infection.

## Symptoms
- **Leaves**: White, powdery, talc-like spots appear on upper and lower leaf surfaces. Spots enlarge and coalesce, eventually covering entire leaves. Severely infected leaves turn yellow, then brown, and die.
- **Stems and Petioles**: White fungal growth may also develop on stems.
- **Fruit**: Rarely directly infected, but premature defoliation leads to sunscald on fruit, reduced sugar content, poor fruit quality, and shortened harvest period.

## Disease Cycle
1. Conidia (asexual spores) are wind-dispersed over long distances — no local overwintering source is required.
2. Spores germinate on leaf surfaces in 3-6 hours; infection does not require free water (unusual for fungi).
3. Optimal conditions: 68-80°F (20-27°C), moderate humidity (50-90%), shady conditions.
4. Rain and overhead irrigation actually suppress powdery mildew by washing spores off leaves.
5. After infection, new conidia are produced in 5-7 days, enabling rapid epidemic development.
6. Late-season infections are most common and severe.

## Treatment

### Chemical Control
- Apply fungicides at the first sign of white patches.
- Effective products: myclobutanil (Rally), chlorothalonil, sulfur, or strobilurin fungicides.
- Alternate chemistries to prevent resistance (powdery mildew fungi develop resistance rapidly, especially to strobilurins).

### Organic Control
- Potassium bicarbonate (e.g., MilStop, Kaligreen) disrupts fungal cell walls; apply at first sign of disease. This is one of the most effective organic options.
- Neem oil provides some suppression; apply in the evening to avoid phototoxicity.
- Sulfur-based fungicides are effective preventatives. **Do not apply when temperatures exceed 90°F (32°C)** — severe leaf burn will result.
- Biological control: *Bacillus amyloliquefaciens* (Double Nickel) or *Bacillus subtilis* (Serenade) applied preventatively.
- **Milk sprays** (10-40% solution of whole or skim milk mixed with water) have shown surprising efficacy in university research trials. The mechanism is not fully understood, but milk proteins appear to have antifungal properties when exposed to sunlight. This is a practical, inexpensive option for home gardeners — spray weekly, covering both leaf surfaces.
- Horticultural oils (plant-based or petroleum) can suppress mildew but must be applied carefully to avoid phytotoxicity in hot weather.

**Real-world tip for home gardeners**: Powdery mildew on squash is one of the most common gardening complaints. The #1 recommendation from university extension programs nationwide is simply to plant resistant varieties — PM-resistant zucchini, summer squash, and winter squash are widely available from all major seed companies. If you grow a susceptible variety, expect mildew by mid-to-late summer; it's nearly inevitable in most climates.

### Cultural Practices
- Ensure good air circulation and avoid overcrowding between plants.
- Plant resistant varieties — PM-resistant squash, pumpkin, and melon cultivars are widely available.
- Maintain plant vigor through balanced fertilization; avoid drought stress.
- Remove and destroy severely infected leaves.
- Water at the base of plants; avoid overhead irrigation late in the day.

## Prevention
- Plant powdery mildew-resistant cultivars — this is the most effective strategy.
- Use recommended plant spacing to ensure air movement through the canopy.
- Scout plants regularly starting at mid-season; early detection is key to effective management.
- Apply preventative fungicides before symptoms appear in areas with a history of the disease.
