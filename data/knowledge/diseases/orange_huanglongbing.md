# Citrus Huanglongbing / Citrus Greening (Candidatus Liberibacter spp.)

## Overview
Huanglongbing (HLB), commonly known as citrus greening disease, is considered the most devastating disease of citrus worldwide. It is caused by the phloem-limited bacterium *Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus* (and related species). The disease is transmitted primarily by the Asian citrus psyllid (*Diaphorina citri*). There is currently no cure. Infected trees decline over several years and eventually become unproductive.

## Symptoms
- **Leaves**: Blotchy, asymmetric yellowing (mottling) of leaves — this is the hallmark symptom. Unlike nutrient deficiency, the yellowing pattern is irregular and does not follow leaf veins symmetrically. Affected leaves are often small, upright, and may show thickened, leathery texture.
- **Fruit**: Lopsided, small, and poorly colored fruit that remains partially green even when mature. Seeds may be aborted. Fruit has bitter, unpleasant taste with low juice quality.
- **Tree**: Twig dieback, sparse canopy, and stunted growth. One section of the tree may show symptoms first ("sectored" symptom expression). Eventually, the entire canopy declines.
- **Roots**: Root decline often precedes above-ground symptoms, as the bacterium disrupts phloem transport.

## Disease Cycle
1. The Asian citrus psyllid acquires the bacterium by feeding on infected citrus trees.
2. After an incubation period (2-3 weeks in the insect), the psyllid can transmit the bacterium for life.
3. The bacterium multiplies in the phloem of the citrus tree, disrupting nutrient transport.
4. Symptoms may take 6 months to several years to appear after infection.
5. The psyllid feeds on new flush growth (tender young shoots) — all citrus species are hosts.

## Treatment

### Chemical Control
- Control Asian citrus psyllid vector with systemic insecticides (imidacloprid, thiamethoxam) applied as soil drenches or foliar sprays.
- Kaolin clay particle film can deter psyllid feeding.
- Foliar sprays targeting adult psyllids: carbaryl, dimethoate, or spinosad.
- Nutritional therapies (enhanced micronutrient programs) may help sustain tree productivity but do not eliminate the pathogen.

### Cultural Practices
- Remove and destroy infected trees promptly to reduce the inoculum source in the area.
- Use certified disease-free nursery stock from screened and enclosed nurseries.
- Coordinate area-wide psyllid management with neighboring groves for better vector control.
- Plant new trees only from certified nurseries that test mother trees for HLB.
- Remove abandoned citrus trees and volunteer seedlings that may harbor psyllids and the pathogen.

### Biological Control
- Release natural enemies of the psyllid: *Tamarixia radiata* (parasitoid wasp) and *Diaphorencyrtus aligarhensis*.
- These biological control agents reduce psyllid populations but do not eliminate them.

## Prevention
- Area-wide coordinated psyllid management is essential — individual grower efforts alone are insufficient.
- Use certified disease-free planting material exclusively.
- Monitor psyllid populations regularly with yellow sticky traps and stem-tap sampling.
- Scout for HLB symptoms regularly; PCR testing can confirm infection before symptoms appear.
- In residential settings, do not move citrus plants between regions.

## Important Note
HLB is a regulated quarantine disease in many countries and U.S. states. According to the USDA Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service (APHIS), five exotic citrus pests and diseases are currently established in the United States with the potential to harm every acre of citrus: huanglongbing (citrus greening), Asian citrus psyllid, citrus canker, citrus black spot, and sweet orange scab.

**If you suspect HLB**: Contact your local agricultural authority or State Plant Health Director immediately. Do not attempt to manage the disease alone. In many states, it is legally required to report suspected HLB.

**For homeowners**: Do not move citrus plants or citrus nursery stock between regions, as this is a primary pathway for disease spread. Purchase citrus only from certified, disease-free nurseries. If you live in a citrus-producing state, inspect your citrus trees regularly and report any symptoms (blotchy yellow leaves, small lopsided fruit, twig dieback) to your county extension office or state agriculture department.

**Economic impact**: HLB has devastated Florida's citrus industry, reducing production by over 70% since the disease was first detected in 2005. There is currently no cure — research into heat treatment, antibiotic injection, and tolerant rootstocks is ongoing but has not yet produced a commercially viable solution.
