# Tomato Bacterial Spot (Xanthomonas spp.)

## Overview
Bacterial spot of tomato is caused by a complex of four *Xanthomonas* species: *X. euvesicatoria*, *X. vesicatoria*, *X. perforans*, and *X. gardneri*. The disease is widespread in warm, humid regions and can cause severe defoliation, reduced fruit quality, and direct fruit infection. It is one of the most challenging bacterial diseases to manage because of increasing copper resistance.

## Symptoms
- **Leaves**: Small (2-3 mm), water-soaked, dark spots that become angular and necrotic. A narrow yellow halo may surround spots. Spots may coalesce, causing large areas of necrosis and leaf drop.
- **Fruit**: Small, raised, scab-like spots develop on green fruit. Spots are initially dark, water-soaked, then become raised with a rough, corky texture. Infected fruit is unmarketable.
- **Stems**: Elongated, dark, raised lesions on stems and petioles.

## Disease Cycle
1. The bacterium is seed-borne and can survive on infected crop debris for months (but survives poorly in soil when not associated with debris).
2. Warm rain and overhead irrigation splash bacteria onto foliage and fruit.
3. Bacteria enter through stomata, hydathodes, and wounds (including wind-driven sand abrasion, insect damage, and even hail injury).
4. Optimal conditions: temperatures 75-86°F (24-30°C) with frequent rain. Wind-driven rain contributes to more severe disease.
5. Multiple secondary cycles per season; severe epidemics develop quickly.
6. **Important real-world note**: Bacterial spot symptoms can be easily confused with bacterial speck (*Pseudomonas syringae*). Speck lesions tend to be smaller and more angular; lab diagnosis may be needed for confirmation.

## Treatment

### Important
A plant with active bacterial spot **cannot be cured**. Management focuses on prevention and limiting spread. Remove severely symptomatic plants from the garden to prevent bacteria from spreading to healthy plants. **Do not eat symptomatic fruit** — while the bacterial pathogens are not human pathogens, the fruit blemishes can provide entry points for human pathogens that could cause illness.

### Chemical Control
- Use copper hydroxide sprays on a 5-7 day schedule during wet periods.
- Combine copper with mancozeb to improve efficacy and manage copper-resistant strains.
- Acibenzolar-S-methyl (Actigard) induces plant systemic resistance; use as part of an integrated program.
- **Critical real-world warning**: Copper-resistant bacterial strains are increasingly prevalent worldwide. If copper sprays seem ineffective after consistent application, resistance in the local population is likely. Do not simply increase copper rates — this causes phytotoxicity without improving control.
- Use copper-based products preventatively, especially during warm, wet periods. Copper is more effective as a protectant than a curative.
- Excessive or prolonged copper use leads to copper accumulation in soil, which is toxic to soil microorganisms.

### Cultural Practices
- Rotate with non-solanaceous crops for 3+ years.
- Use pathogen-free seed. If a clean seed source is not available, treat seed with hot water: soak at 122°F (50°C) for 25 minutes to eliminate surface and internal bacteria. Let seeds dry thoroughly before planting.
- Use disease-free transplants from reputable nurseries. Inspect transplants carefully before planting.
- **Avoid overhead irrigation** — use drip tape or soaker hoses to keep leaves dry.
- Do not work in fields when foliage is wet (from rain, dew, or irrigation) to prevent spreading bacteria on hands, tools, and clothing.
- Routinely sterilize pruning tools and stakes with 70% isopropyl alcohol (preferred — less corrosive than bleach) or 10% bleach solution between plants.
- Remove and destroy crop residue immediately after final harvest — burn (where allowed), bury, or hot compost.
- Stake and trellis plants to improve air circulation and reduce leaf wetness duration.
- Remove any volunteer tomato plants the following season — they can harbor the pathogen.

## Prevention
- Seed treatment (hot water or acidified cupric acetate) is critical for seed-borne inoculum reduction.
- Use resistant or tolerant cultivars when available.
- Inspect transplants carefully before setting in the field.
- Avoid smoking or using tobacco products near tomato plants (for TMV awareness, not bacterial spot, but general sanitation matters).
