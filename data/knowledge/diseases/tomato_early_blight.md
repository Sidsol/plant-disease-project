# Tomato Early Blight (Alternaria solani)

## Overview
Early blight of tomato is caused by *Alternaria solani* (and sometimes *A. tomatophila*). It is one of the most common tomato diseases worldwide, occurring in nearly every region where tomatoes are grown. The disease affects leaves, stems, and fruit, and can significantly reduce yield through defoliation and direct fruit infection.

## Symptoms
- **Leaves**: Dark brown, circular lesions (0.5-1.5 cm) with characteristic concentric rings forming a "target spot" or "bull's-eye" pattern. Lesions often have a yellow halo. Lower, older leaves are affected first; disease progresses upward.
- **Stems**: Dark, elongated lesions develop on stems at or near the soil line ("collar rot" in seedlings).
- **Fruit**: Large, leathery, dark brown to black lesions develop on the stem end of the fruit, often with concentric rings. Green and ripe fruit can both be infected.

## Disease Cycle
1. The fungus overwinters in infected plant debris on the soil surface and in/on tomato seed.
2. Conidia are produced on debris in spring, splashed or wind-carried to lower leaves.
3. Alternating wet and dry periods with moderate warmth (75-85°F / 24-29°C) favor infection.
4. The incubation period is 3-5 days; secondary cycles repeat every 5-7 days.
5. Stressed, nutrient-deficient, or older plants are more susceptible.

## Treatment

### Chemical Control
- Apply chlorothalonil or mancozeb at first symptoms; continue on a 7-10 day schedule.
- Azoxystrobin, boscalid, or difenoconazole provide systemic control.
- Alternate fungicide classes to prevent resistance.

### Cultural Practices
- Mulch around plants with 2-4 inches of straw, wood chips, or plastic mulch to prevent soil splashing onto lower leaves — this is one of the most effective single-step cultural controls. Soil splash carries spores from overwintered debris to lower foliage.
- Remove lower infected leaves promptly, especially the bottom 12 inches of foliage. This slows upward progression.
- Rotate with non-solanaceous crops for 3+ years. The pathogen also infects potato, pepper, and eggplant — rotating tomato with pepper provides no disease benefit.
- Maintain adequate fertility, especially nitrogen and potassium. **Nutrient-deficient plants are dramatically more susceptible** — a well-fed plant resists early blight far better than a starved one.
- Stake or cage plants to keep foliage off the ground and improve air flow.
- Water at the base using drip irrigation. **Never use overhead sprinklers on tomatoes** — this is the single worst watering mistake for disease management.
- Remove all crop debris at end of season — don't till debris under; remove it from the garden entirely.

**Real-world tip**: In humid climates (Southeast US, Mid-Atlantic), early blight is essentially inevitable on susceptible varieties by mid-season. Plan your management strategy before planting, not after symptoms appear. Start fungicide applications or cultural controls proactively.

### Organic Control
- Copper-based fungicides provide moderate protection.
- *Bacillus subtilis* (Serenade) or *Bacillus amyloliquefaciens* may reduce severity.
- Compost tea has shown some efficacy in research trials.

## Prevention
- Plant resistant or tolerant varieties when available (Mountain Merit, Defiant PhR, Iron Lady).
- Use certified disease-free or hot-water-treated seed.
- Remove all crop debris at end of season.
- Avoid planting tomatoes in the same location in consecutive years.
