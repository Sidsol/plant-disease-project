# Tomato Late Blight (Phytophthora infestans)

## Overview
Late blight of tomato is caused by the oomycete *Phytophthora infestans*, the same pathogen that causes late blight of potato. It is one of the most destructive tomato diseases, capable of destroying an entire planting within days under favorable conditions. The disease affects leaves, stems, and fruit.

## Symptoms
- **Leaves**: Large, water-soaked, pale green to dark brown lesions expand rapidly, often starting at leaf tips and edges. Under humid conditions, white fuzzy sporulation is visible on the lower leaf surface.
- **Stems**: Dark, water-soaked, firm lesions girdle stems and branches, causing wilting and collapse of tissue above the lesion.
- **Fruit**: Large, firm, greasy-looking, brown lesions develop on green or ripe fruit, often on the upper surface. White sporulation may develop on fruit lesions under humid conditions.

## Disease Cycle
1. The pathogen does not survive in soil or crop debris very long but can overwinter in infected potato tubers, volunteer plants, or greenhouse tomatoes.
2. Wind-dispersed sporangia travel long distances (miles) and initiate new infections.
3. Under cool conditions (<59°F / 15°C), zoospores are released, enhancing infection.
4. Only 3-5 days from infection to symptom appearance.
5. Cool (50-70°F / 10-21°C), wet, overcast weather is ideal for explosive epidemics.

## Treatment

### Chemical Control
- Apply metalaxyl (Ridomil) or chlorothalonil preventively during wet seasons.
- Cymoxanil + mancozeb or fluopicolide provide effective control.
- Monitor for metalaxyl-resistant strains in your region.
- Spray intervals: 5-7 days during active disease; 10-14 days during dry, low-risk conditions.

### Cultural Practices
- Destroy infected plant material immediately — bag and dispose in trash; **do NOT compost** late blight-infected material. The pathogen is too aggressive for most home compost systems.
- Use resistant varieties: Mountain Magic, Mountain Merit, Plum Regal, Iron Lady, Defiant PhR. These won't be immune to all strains, but they slow disease development significantly.
- Avoid overhead irrigation; use drip systems.
- Stake plants and prune lower branches for air circulation.
- Plant tomatoes away from potato fields or potato gardens — both crops are hosts and can cross-infect.
- Monitor late blight tracking systems (e.g., USABlight.org) for regional alerts.
- **Timing matters enormously**: At the first sign of late blight, you have 3-5 days to act before the disease becomes uncontrollable. Don't wait to see if it "gets worse" — by then it's too late.

**Real-world context for home gardeners**: Late blight is the one tomato disease that demands immediate, aggressive action. Unlike early blight or septoria (which slowly defoliate plants over weeks), late blight kills plants in days and produces vast numbers of spores that can drift for miles. If you see late blight in your garden, remove and bag ALL affected plants, even if they're still producing fruit. You're protecting both your remaining plants and your neighbors' gardens.

### Organic Control
- Copper-based fungicides applied preventatively are the primary organic option.
- Must apply before disease onset; copper has limited curative activity.

## Prevention
- Use late blight monitoring and forecasting systems.
- Remove volunteer potato and tomato plants.
- Avoid purchasing transplants from sources with unknown disease status.
- Report outbreaks to local extension services.
