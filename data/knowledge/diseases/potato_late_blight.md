# Potato Late Blight (Phytophthora infestans)

## Overview
Late blight of potato, caused by the oomycete *Phytophthora infestans*, is historically one of the most devastating plant diseases. It was the causal agent of the Irish Potato Famine (1845-1852). The disease can destroy an entire field within days under favorable conditions. It affects both foliage and tubers and remains globally significant with losses estimated in the billions of dollars annually.

## Symptoms
- **Leaves**: Large (>1 cm), water-soaked, pale green to dark brown lesions develop rapidly, often starting at leaf tips or edges. Under humid conditions, white, fluffy sporangia are visible on the lower leaf surface at the lesion margins.
- **Stems**: Dark brown to black lesions encircle stems, causing them to collapse.
- **Tubers**: Irregular, slightly depressed, reddish-brown patches on the skin. When cut, infected tissue shows a dry, granular, reddish-brown discoloration extending into the tuber flesh.
- **Speed**: Under favorable conditions (cool, wet), lesions expand rapidly and an entire field can be destroyed within 7-10 days.

## Disease Cycle
1. The pathogen overwinters in infected tubers (in storage, cull piles, or volunteer plants).
2. Infected tubers produce infected shoots in spring; sporangia form on symptomatic foliage.
3. Sporangia are wind-dispersed for miles and can also be rain-splashed.
4. Under cool conditions (<59°F / 15°C), sporangia release motile zoospores that swim in water films to infect.
5. Under warmer conditions, sporangia germinate directly.
6. The incubation period is only 3-5 days; multiple disease cycles per season.
7. Tuber infection occurs when sporangia wash into soil and contact tubers.

## Treatment

### Chemical Control
- Apply preventive fungicide (chlorothalonil, mancozeb) before wet weather events.
- Systemic fungicides: mefenoxam (Ridomil Gold), cymoxanil, or fluopicolide for curative action.
- Monitor for mefenoxam-resistant strains (common in some regions); incorporate non-resistant chemistries.
- Maintain a 5-7 day spray interval during active disease; 7-14 days during low-risk periods.
- Late-season applications protect tubers from infection at harvest.

### Cultural Practices
- Destroy volunteer potatoes and cull piles that harbor the pathogen over winter.
- Plant certified disease-free seed potatoes from reputable sources.
- Plant late-blight resistant varieties when available (e.g., Defender, Elba, Jacqueline Lee).
- Hill potatoes deeply to protect tubers from surface spore infiltration.
- Harvest during dry weather; allow tuber skins to set before storage.
- Kill vines (desiccation) 2-3 weeks before harvest to prevent spore transfer to tubers.

### Organic Control
- Copper-based fungicides (Bordeaux mixture) applied preventatively.
- Maintain vigilant monitoring; organic control options are limited once disease establishes.

## Prevention
- Use disease forecasting systems (BLITECAST, Simcast) to time fungicide applications.
- Monitor weather: periods of cool (50-70°F / 10-21°C), wet, cloudy weather are highest risk.
- Avoid planting near tomato fields (tomato is also a host).
- Report suspected outbreaks to extension services; late blight is tracked regionally.

## Important Note
Late blight is an aggressive, community disease. One unmanaged field can produce billions of sporangia that spread for miles. Coordinated, area-wide management is essential — individual grower efforts alone are insufficient.

**Real-world context**: In the United States, late blight destroyed an estimated $7.3 billion worth of potatoes globally in a single year. The disease moves so fast that fields can go from first symptoms to total crop loss in 7-10 days under cool, wet conditions. Many states have active late blight tracking programs (e.g., USABlight at usablight.org) that allow growers to report outbreaks and receive regional alerts.

**For home gardeners**: If you see large, water-soaked lesions with white fuzz on your potato or tomato plants during cool, rainy weather, act immediately. Bag and dispose of affected plants in the trash — do NOT compost them. Alert your local extension office, as your infected plants could be producing spores that travel to neighboring farms.
