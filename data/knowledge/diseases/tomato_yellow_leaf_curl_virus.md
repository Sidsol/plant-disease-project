# Tomato Yellow Leaf Curl Virus (TYLCV)

## Overview
Tomato yellow leaf curl virus (TYLCV) is a begomovirus transmitted by the whitefly *Bemisia tabaci* (silverleaf whitefly). It is one of the most devastating tomato viruses worldwide, particularly in tropical, subtropical, and Mediterranean climates. Yield losses can reach 100% when plants are infected early. The virus has spread dramatically since the 1990s and is now found on every continent where tomatoes are grown.

## Symptoms
- **Leaves**: Upward curling (cupping) of leaf margins, yellowing of leaf edges, and reduced leaf size. Leaflets may become crumpled and thickened. The plant takes on a bushy, stunted appearance.
- **Growth**: Severe stunting, shortened internodes, and excessive branching. Plants infected before flowering typically set no fruit.
- **Flowers**: Flower drop is common; flowers that persist often fail to set fruit.
- **Fruit**: If fruit does set, it may be small and of poor quality. Plants infected after fruit set may produce a partial crop.

## Disease Cycle
1. The virus is transmitted by the whitefly *Bemisia tabaci* (biotype B/MEAM1 and biotype Q/MED).
2. Whiteflies acquire the virus by feeding on infected plants for 15-30 minutes; after a latent period (8-24 hours), they transmit it for life.
3. The virus is not seed-transmitted or mechanically transmitted.
4. Infected transplants moved from greenhouses to fields are a major means of long-distance spread.
5. Weed hosts and other crop hosts (pepper, bean, tobacco) can serve as virus reservoirs.

## Treatment

### Vector Control
- Control whitefly populations using reflective mulches (silver or aluminum) that disorient whiteflies.
- Insecticidal soap or neem oil applications target immature whiteflies on leaf undersides.
- Systemic insecticides (imidacloprid, thiamethoxam) applied as soil drenches at transplanting.
- Yellow sticky traps can monitor and reduce adult whitefly populations in greenhouses.
- Fine-mesh (50-mesh or finer) insect-exclusion screens on greenhouse openings.

### Cultural Practices
- Use TYLCV-resistant tomato cultivars — this is the most effective management strategy. Many modern varieties carry the Ty-1, Ty-2, Ty-3, or Ty-5 resistance genes.
- Remove and destroy infected plants early to reduce the virus reservoir.
- Transplant-free periods: avoid overlapping plantings that bridge whitefly generations.
- Weed management: remove known weed hosts (jimsonweed, nightshade) from around growing areas.
- Avoid planting new tomato crops adjacent to older, infected ones.

## Prevention
- Plant resistant varieties — multiple commercial cultivars with TYLCV resistance are available.
- Use virus-free transplants from screened nurseries.
- Implement whitefly management programs early and consistently.
- Region-wide coordination of planting and destruction dates can break the virus cycle.
- In high-risk regions, use row covers or net houses during the seedling to early vegetative stage.

## Important Note
TYLCV is a regulated quarantine pest in many regions. Report new occurrences to local plant protection authorities.

**Real-world context**: TYLCV has spread dramatically since the 1990s and is now found on every continent where tomatoes are grown commercially. In Florida and the Caribbean, TYLCV is considered the most economically important tomato virus. Yield losses can reach 100% when plants are infected before flowering.

**For home gardeners**: If you notice your tomato plants developing stiff, crumpled leaves that curl strongly upward with yellowed edges, and the plant seems stunted with shortened internodes, check the undersides of leaves for tiny white whiteflies. TYLCV-resistant varieties (look for "Ty" in the variety description) are the most reliable solution. Popular resistant varieties include Tygress, Shanty, and many newer greenhouse varieties. Once a plant is infected, removing it promptly protects neighboring plants by reducing the virus reservoir for whiteflies.

**Distinguishing from other leaf curl**: Not all tomato leaf curling is TYLCV. Physiological leaf curl from heat stress, herbicide drift (especially 2,4-D), and overwatering can cause similar-looking symptoms. TYLCV is distinguished by: (1) the presence of whiteflies, (2) stunted new growth, (3) flower drop, and (4) a bushy, compact plant habit.
