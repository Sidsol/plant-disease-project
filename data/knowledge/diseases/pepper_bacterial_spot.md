# Bell Pepper Bacterial Spot (Xanthomonas euvesicatoria)

## Overview
Bacterial spot of bell pepper is caused by several species within the *Xanthomonas* complex, primarily *X. euvesicatoria* and *X. vesicatoria*. The disease is one of the most economically important bacterial diseases of pepper, causing leaf spots, defoliation, and fruit lesions that reduce yield and fruit quality. It is particularly severe in warm, humid, and rainy conditions.

## Symptoms
- **Leaves**: Small (2-5 mm), dark, water-soaked spots appear on the lower leaf surface. Spots become raised and scab-like as they age. Severely infected leaves turn yellow and drop.
- **Fruit**: Small, raised, wart-like, scab lesions develop on the fruit surface. Lesions may crack and provide entry points for secondary rot organisms.
- **Stems**: Dark, elongated cankers may appear on stems and petioles.
- **Seedlings**: Damping-off and seedling blighting can occur when contaminated seed is planted.

## Disease Cycle
1. The bacterium is seed-borne and can also persist on infected crop debris.
2. Warm rain and overhead irrigation splash bacteria onto new growth.
3. Bacteria enter through stomata and wounds (insect feeding, wind-driven sand abrasion).
4. Optimal conditions: temperatures above 75°F (24°C) with frequent rainfall or overhead irrigation.
5. Once established, the disease can spread rapidly through a field.

## Treatment

### Chemical Control
- Use certified disease-free seed and transplants as the foundation of management.
- Apply copper-based bactericide on a 7-10 day schedule during wet periods.
- Combine copper with mancozeb for improved efficacy (mancozeb enhances copper activity).
- Acibenzolar-S-methyl (Actigard) can be used as a plant defense activator.
- Note: copper-resistant bacterial strains are increasingly common.

### Cultural Practices
- Rotate with non-solanaceous crops for 2-3 years between pepper/tomato plantings. Note that the bacterial spot pathogen is shared between peppers and tomatoes — rotating pepper with tomato provides **no disease benefit**.
- Destroy crop residue promptly after harvest — burn (where permitted), bury, or hot compost.
- **Avoid overhead irrigation** — this is the single most important cultural practice. Use drip or furrow irrigation exclusively.
- Space plants adequately (18-24 inches) for air circulation.
- Avoid working in fields when foliage is wet from rain, dew, or irrigation.
- Use raised beds and plastic mulch to reduce soil splash.
- Sterilize pruning tools and stakes between plants with 70% isopropyl alcohol.

**Real-world tip for home gardeners**: If bacterial spot appears in your pepper patch during a rainy period, the honest truth is that cultural controls alone rarely stop an active epidemic. Focus on protecting next year's crop: plan your rotation, source clean seed, and switch to drip irrigation before planting.

## Prevention
- Start with certified disease-free or hot-water-treated seed.
- Transplant only healthy, symptom-free seedlings.
- Apply preventative copper sprays beginning soon after transplanting.
- Monitor fields regularly and remove heavily infected plants.
