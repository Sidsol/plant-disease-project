# Grape Esca / Black Measles (Phaeomoniella chlamydospora and others)

## Overview
Esca (also known as Black Measles or grapevine trunk disease) is a complex of fungal diseases affecting grapevine trunks and cordons. It involves multiple pathogens including *Phaeomoniella chlamydospora*, *Phaeoacremonium minimum*, and various *Botryosphaeria* species. Esca is one of the most destructive and difficult-to-manage vine diseases worldwide, causing gradual vine decline or sudden apoplectic death.

## Symptoms
- **Leaves**: Interveinal chlorosis and necrosis in a characteristic "tiger stripe" pattern. Brown and yellow bands develop between the veins while tissue near veins remains green. In white varieties, yellowing is predominant; in red varieties, reddish-brown discoloration appears.
- **Fruit**: Dark, purplish-brown spots or cracks appear on berry surfaces ("measles" symptom). Berries may shrivel or split.
- **Trunk/Cordons**: When cross-sectioned, affected wood shows light-colored, soft, spongy decay (white rot) and/or dark brown to black streaking in the vascular tissue.
- **Apoplexy**: In hot weather, entire vines or arms may wilt and die suddenly within days ("apoplectic form").

## Disease Cycle
1. Fungi colonize pruning wounds and natural openings, growing slowly through the vascular system over years.
2. Spores are produced during wet weather and spread via rain splash and wind.
3. Infection can remain latent for years before symptoms appear — vines may show symptoms one year and not the next ("chronic form").
4. Hot, dry conditions during summer stress may trigger sudden symptom expression.
5. Older vineyards (10+ years) are more commonly affected.

## Treatment

### Chemical Control
- No curative chemical treatment currently exists for esca.
- Pruning wound protectants (trichoderma-based pastes, or chemical wound sealants containing thiophanate-methyl or boric acid) can reduce new infections.
- Sodium arsenite was historically used but is now banned in most countries due to toxicity.

### Cultural Practices
- Remove severely affected vines promptly to reduce inoculum. Burn or remove infected wood from the vineyard.
- Retrain vines from suckers below the affected area if the rootstock is healthy.
- Avoid heavy pruning during wet weather — prune during dry periods to reduce spore dispersal.
- Make clean, angled pruning cuts that promote rapid wound healing.
- Delay pruning as late as possible in the dormant season when spore loads are lower.
- Double pruning (rough cut in winter, final cut in spring) can reduce infection.

### Organic/Biological Control
- Apply *Trichoderma*-based products (e.g., Trichodex, T-77) to pruning wounds immediately after cutting.
- These biological agents colonize wounds before pathogenic fungi can establish.

## Prevention
- Protect pruning wounds within 24 hours of cutting using approved wound protectants.
- Minimize large wounds — avoid removing large-diameter wood when possible.
- Use certified, pathogen-free nursery stock when establishing new vineyards.
- Maintain vine vigor through balanced nutrition and irrigation.
