# Tomato Spider Mites / Two-Spotted Spider Mite (Tetranychus urticae)

## Overview
Two-spotted spider mite (*Tetranychus urticae*) is one of the most polyphagous arthropod herbivores, attacking over 1,100 plant species including tomato. While not a disease but a pest, spider mite infestations cause damage that is often initially mistaken for a disease. Heavy infestations can cause severe leaf damage, reduced photosynthesis, and significant yield loss.

## Symptoms
- **Leaves**: Fine, light-colored speckling (stippling) on the upper leaf surface, caused by mites puncturing cells and feeding on contents. Leaves gradually turn bronze, yellow, and eventually dry and brown.
- **Webbing**: Fine silk webs on the undersides of leaves and between leaflets, especially when populations are high.
- **Plant-wide**: In severe infestations, leaves become desiccated, flowers may abort, and fruit quality (size, sugar content) is reduced.
- **Mites**: Tiny (< 0.5 mm), oval, yellowish-green arthropods with two dark spots on the body. Visible with a hand lens on the underside of leaves.

## Pest Cycle
1. Adult mites overwinter in soil debris, on weeds, or in greenhouse structures.
2. Females begin laying eggs on host plants in spring; each female can produce 100+ eggs.
3. Development from egg to adult is rapid (5-20 days depending on temperature).
4. Hot, dry conditions (above 80°F / 27°C with low humidity) strongly favor population explosions.
5. Dusty conditions knock natural predators off balance while mites persist.
6. Multiple overlapping generations occur per season; populations can increase 10-fold in 1-2 weeks.

## Treatment

### Biological Control (Preferred)
- Release predatory mites (*Phytoseiulus persimilis*) — the most effective biological control agent. They consume spider mites faster than the mites reproduce under ideal conditions.
- *Neoseiulus californicus* is more heat- and drought-tolerant than *P. persimilis*.
- *Stethorus punctillum* (spider mite destroyer beetle) feeds voraciously on spider mites.
- Lacewing larvae (*Chrysoperla* spp.) are generalist predators that also consume mites.

### Organic Control
- Apply insecticidal soap directly to undersides of leaves where mites feed. Thorough coverage is essential.
- Neem oil disrupts mite feeding and reproduction.
- Horticultural oils (petroleum or plant-based) suffocate mites and eggs.
- Increase ambient humidity around plants to discourage mite reproduction.
- Water-spray the undersides of leaves to physically dislodge mites.

### Chemical Control (Last Resort)
- Abamectin, spiromesifen, or bifenazate are effective miticides.
- Avoid broad-spectrum insecticides (pyrethroids, organophosphates) which kill beneficial predatory mites and can cause mite resurgence.
- Rotate miticide classes to prevent resistance development.

### Cultural Practices
- Increase humidity around plants (misting, proper irrigation) to discourage mite reproduction.
- Remove and destroy heavily infested leaves.
- Control dust on roads and pathways near growing areas.
- Keep weeds down around the growing area, as they serve as alternate hosts.
- Avoid over-fertilization with nitrogen, which produces lush growth attractive to mites.

## Prevention
- Regularly inspect undersides of leaves with a hand lens (10x), especially during hot, dry weather. Spider mites are nearly invisible to the naked eye — look for stippling damage and fine webbing.
- **Early detection test**: Hold a white piece of paper under a suspect leaf and tap sharply. If tiny specks fall onto the paper and start moving, those are spider mites.
- Establish predatory mites as a preventative measure early in the season in greenhouse or high-tunnel growing.
- Maintain adequate irrigation and humidity — drought-stressed plants are mite magnets.
- Avoid practices that suppress natural enemies — **this is the #1 real-world mistake**: broad-spectrum insecticide sprays (especially pyrethroids like permethrin and bifenthrin) kill predatory mites and beneficial insects while barely affecting spider mites, causing explosive mite outbreaks 2-3 weeks after spraying. This is called "pesticide-induced pest resurgence" and is extremely common.

**Real-world tip**: Many home gardeners blame spider mite outbreaks on hot weather alone, but the most common cause is actually a previous insecticide application that wiped out natural predators. If you sprayed a pyrethroid insecticide for another pest and then noticed spider mites 2-3 weeks later, the spray caused the mite problem. The solution is to stop pyrethroid sprays and let predator populations recover, or release predatory mites.
