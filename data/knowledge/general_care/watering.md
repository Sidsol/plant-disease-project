# Watering Best Practices for Plant Health

## General Principles
Proper watering is one of the most important factors in preventing both disease and stress in plants. Overwatering leads to root rot and fungal diseases, while underwatering stresses plants and makes them more susceptible to pests and pathogens.

## Watering Guidelines by Plant Type

### Fruit Trees (Apple, Cherry, Peach, Citrus)
- Deep, infrequent watering encourages deep root systems. Water thoroughly every 7-14 days during dry periods.
- Young trees (1-3 years) need more frequent watering than established trees.
- Apply 10-15 gallons per inch of trunk diameter per watering session.
- Water at the drip line (outer edge of canopy), not directly at the trunk.
- Reduce watering in fall to harden off growth before winter.

### Vegetables (Tomato, Pepper, Potato, Squash)
- Tomatoes: 1-2 inches per week. Water deeply and consistently; fluctuating moisture causes blossom end rot and fruit cracking.
- Peppers: 1-1.5 inches per week. Consistent moisture is critical during flowering and fruit set.
- Potatoes: 1-2 inches per week. Consistent moisture during tuber formation (bloom stage) is critical. Excessive moisture promotes late blight.
- Squash/Cucurbits: 1-1.5 inches per week. Water at the base to keep foliage dry.

### Small Fruits (Strawberry, Raspberry, Blueberry, Grape)
- Strawberries: 1-1.5 inches per week. Avoid overhead watering during fruit development.
- Grapes: Established vines are drought-tolerant. Water deeply but infrequently. Deficit irrigation can improve fruit quality.
- Raspberries: 1-1.5 inches per week. Moisture is critical during fruiting.

### Field Crops (Corn, Soybean)
- Corn: 1 inch per week minimum; 1.5 inches during tasseling/silking (peak water demand).
- Soybeans: 1-1.5 inches per week during pod fill.

## Disease Prevention Through Watering
- **Use drip irrigation or soaker hoses** whenever possible. Overhead irrigation wets foliage, promoting foliar diseases. University extension programs consistently rank switching from overhead to drip irrigation as the single highest-impact cultural change a gardener can make for disease prevention.
- **Water in the morning** so foliage dries quickly. Evening watering leaves moisture on leaves overnight — most fungal spores germinate in 6-12 hours of continuous moisture, and overnight watering easily provides this window.
- **Mulch around plants** (2-4 inches of straw, wood chips, or shredded leaves) to retain soil moisture, reduce splashing of soil-borne pathogens onto lower leaves, and moderate soil temperature. Plastic mulch (black or silver) is even more effective for commercial vegetable production.
- **Avoid waterlogging** — ensure proper drainage. Standing water promotes root diseases (Phytophthora, Pythium) that can kill plants within days. If your soil is heavy clay, consider raised beds.
- **Maintain consistent moisture** — drought stress weakens plant defenses and makes them more susceptible to disease; sudden heavy watering after drought can cause fruit cracking (especially in tomatoes) and blossom end rot.
- **Real-world mistake to avoid**: Many gardeners water lightly every day. This creates a shallow root zone that is more susceptible to drought stress and disease. Instead, water deeply (saturating the top 6-8 inches of soil) 2-3 times per week rather than shallowly every day.

## Signs of Watering Problems
### Overwatering
- Yellowing lower leaves, wilting despite wet soil, root rot (brown/mushy roots), fungus gnats, algae on soil surface.
### Underwatering
- Wilting (recovers when watered), dry/crispy leaf edges, slow growth, premature flower/fruit drop, blossom end rot in tomatoes.
