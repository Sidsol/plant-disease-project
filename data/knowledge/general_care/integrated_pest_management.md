# Integrated Pest Management (IPM) for Plant Disease Prevention

## What is IPM?
Integrated Pest Management (IPM) is a science-based, sustainable approach to managing pests and diseases. It combines cultural, biological, mechanical, and chemical methods to minimize crop damage while reducing environmental impact. The goal is not zero pests, but keeping pest populations below economically damaging levels.

## The IPM Pyramid (Priority Order)

### 1. Prevention (Foundation)
- Select disease-resistant cultivars — the most cost-effective strategy.
- Use certified disease-free seed and planting material.
- Site selection: good drainage, air circulation, sun exposure.
- Proper spacing for air movement.
- Crop rotation (3-4 year minimum for most pathogens).
- Sanitation: remove crop debris, clean tools, manage weeds.

### 2. Cultural Controls
- Adjust planting dates to avoid peak disease/pest pressure.
- Proper irrigation (drip preferred; morning watering).
- Balanced fertilization (avoid excess nitrogen).
- Mulching to prevent soil splash and conserve moisture.
- Pruning for air circulation and light penetration.
- Trap cropping and companion planting.

### 3. Biological Controls
- Encourage natural enemies: predatory mites, parasitoid wasps, lacewings.
- Apply microbial biopesticides: *Bacillus subtilis*, *Bacillus thuringiensis* (Bt), *Trichoderma* spp.
- Use compost and compost tea to boost beneficial soil microorganisms.
- Preserve habitat for beneficial insects (flower strips, hedgerows).

### 4. Mechanical/Physical Controls
- Hand-picking of pests and diseased plant parts.
- Row covers and insect-exclusion netting.
- Sticky traps for monitoring and mass trapping.
- Reflective mulches to repel certain insects.
- Soil solarization for soil-borne pathogen control.

### 5. Chemical Controls (Last Resort)
- Use only when monitoring indicates thresholds are exceeded.
- Select the least toxic effective option.
- Use targeted, specific products rather than broad-spectrum.
- Rotate modes of action (FRAC/IRAC groups) to prevent resistance.
- Follow label directions precisely.
- Observe pre-harvest intervals and re-entry intervals.

## Scouting and Monitoring
- Inspect plants at least weekly during the growing season.
- Check both upper and lower leaf surfaces.
- Monitor environmental conditions (temperature, humidity, rainfall) — diseases are weather-driven.
- Use disease forecasting tools when available (e.g., Mills table for apple scab, BLITECAST for late blight).
- Keep records of pest/disease occurrences, weather, and management actions.

## Action Thresholds
- Not every pest or lesion requires treatment. Consider:
  - Is the pest/disease increasing or decreasing?
  - What is the crop's growth stage and economic value?
  - What are the weather forecasts (will conditions favor or suppress disease)?
  - Are natural enemies present and active?
